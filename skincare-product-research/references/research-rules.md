# Research and Browsing Rules

Source handling, marketing contamination control, evidence language, and terminology rules for the `skincare-product-research` skill.

For product evaluation and recommendations, current research is normally required because formulations, prices, availability, and regulations change.

## Source hierarchy

Prefer:

1. official formulation / regulatory source;
2. peer-reviewed medical or cosmetic-science literature;
3. dermatology organizations and clinical guidelines;
4. reputable ingredient databases for cross-checking INCI identities;
5. major retailers for current pricing and package size;
6. user communities for subjective experience only.

Cross-check ingredient lists when possible because brand pages and retailers may lag after reformulation.

## Marketing contamination control

When using a source, identify whether it is:

- manufacturer-controlled;
- retailer-controlled;
- affiliate-supported;
- editorial;
- academic;
- regulatory;
- community-generated.

Brand sources are authoritative for what the brand claims and often for ingredient lists, but not independent proof that the claims are clinically meaningful.

## Evidence language

Use calibrated language:

- "well supported"
- "moderately supported"
- "plausible but limited clinical evidence"
- "primarily formulation logic"
- "supported only by brand testing"
- "marketing framing exceeds the evidence"
- "not enough evidence to conclude"

Avoid categorical claims such as "this ingredient does nothing" unless evidence truly supports that statement.

## Terminology rules

When introducing an abbreviation for the first time, expand it once.

Examples:

- **TEWL — Transepidermal Water Loss**, 经表皮水分流失;
- **AHA — Alpha Hydroxy Acid**, α-羟基酸;
- **BHA — Beta Hydroxy Acid**, β-羟基酸;
- **BPO — Benzoyl Peroxide**, 过氧化苯甲酰;
- **OTC — Over The Counter**, 非处方药;
- **INCI — International Nomenclature of Cosmetic Ingredients**, 国际化妆品原料命名;
- **PIH — Post-Inflammatory Hyperpigmentation**, 炎症后色素沉着;
- **PIE — Post-Inflammatory Erythema**, 炎症后红斑.

When a chemical or product-category name has an informative origin, explain it briefly if doing so improves understanding.

Examples:

- "surfactant" = surface-active agent;
- "syndet" = synthetic detergent;
- retinol / retinal / retinoic acid nomenclature reflects alcohol / aldehyde / acid oxidation states.

Do not add etymology merely for decoration.
